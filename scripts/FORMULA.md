# Dog Shorts formula (v3): science first, tuned by our numbers, built to break 100K

Read this before writing a script. It is a starting point, not a cage: the goal is 100K+ views per video, and our
last 6 explainers all landed at 23–35K, so we have to try things. Section 6 says which rules are fixed and which are
experiments.

## 1. The science we stand on (every claim in a script must come from here or a new entry)

Findings below were checked against abstracts and press coverage in this session, not the full papers. Check the
paper again before quoting an exact number on screen.

| Video | Study | What it showed (safe claim) | Do not say |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stare | Nagasawa et al. 2015, *Science* | Longer owner–dog gazing went with higher oxytocin in both (urine oxytocin); hand-raised wolves did not show the loop (later commentary questions that dog-wolf comparison). | "Only dogs can do this", "he is hugging you" as fact (it is a metaphor). |
| Yawn | Joly-Mascheroni et al. 2008, *Biology Letters*; Romero et al. 2013, *PLoS ONE* | 21 of 29 dogs yawned after a person yawned, none after mouth movements; more yawns for the owner than a stranger. | "Proves empathy", "all dogs yawn". A 2009 follow-up found weaker evidence. |
| Guilty look | Horowitz 2009, *Behavioural Processes* | 14 dogs: the "guilty look" followed the owner's scolding, not whether the dog disobeyed. | That the dog "feels nothing"; show a calm owner, never an angry one. |
| Eyes | Kaminski et al. 2019, *PNAS*; Waller et al. 2013, *PLoS ONE* | Dogs have an inner-eyebrow muscle (LAOM) that wolves lack (6 dogs, 4 wolves); shelter dogs raising the inner brow were rehomed faster. | "Evolved to manipulate you". A later study links the brow raise to eye movements. |
| Tears | Murata et al. 2022, *Current Biology* | Tear volume rose about 10 % when dogs met their owner after 5–7 h, not for familiar non-owners; oxytocin on the eye also raised it. | "Crying from sadness". It is tear volume, not proof of emotion. |
| Toy, Bathroom, Welcome | none on file | Written from common owner experience. | Do not add a number or "scientists" unless a source is added here. |

## 2. Why people watch and share (viewer psychology we use)

- **Curiosity gap** (Loewenstein 1994, *Psychological Bulletin*): curiosity is the discomfort of a gap between what you
  know and what you want to know. The gap must feel *closable* and not be too big or too small. So: open with a question
  the viewer half knows ("why does he stare?") and withhold the answer for a few seconds, not for the whole video.
- **Arousal drives sharing** (Berger & Milkman 2012, *J. Marketing Research*, ~7,000 NYT articles plus experiments):
  high-arousal emotions (awe, amusement, anger, anxiety) spread more; low-arousal sadness spreads less, even when
  controlling for how surprising or useful the content was. Our two sad stories got 4K and our warm/"you" videos 23–35K,
  which fits (it does not prove it). So: aim every video at **awe, amusement or a warm jolt**, never a quiet sad ending.
- **Test it now** (practical value, a shareable "try it on your dog"): "Watch your dog" works because the viewer can
  check it in 5 seconds. Keep a testable moment in every script.
- **A fact worth repeating**: people send things that make them look knowing ("dogs can tell how long you've been gone").
  Put one repeatable line in each video.

## 3. Platform facts that change how we read our numbers

- Since **31 March 2025** a Short's view counts when it *starts or replays*, with no minimum watch time. The old
  "stayed to watch" count is now **engaged views** (Studio → Advanced mode). So our view totals include fast swipes, and
  **replays add views directly** (a clean loop can raise views). It also means "% viewed above 100 %" can no longer be
  used to prove a loop works; the v2 note saying so was wrong.
- YouTube has not published a retention or "swiped away" threshold. *Viewed vs swiped away* is a diagnostic for the
  hook, not a known ranking number. Compare each video with our own earlier ones.
- Most Shorts traffic comes from the Shorts feed; our impressions (80–443 per video) confirm that titles and thumbnails
  barely matter here.

## 4. What our numbers say (Oct 7–26, 2026)

Eight dog videos made 99.8 % of the 178,592 channel views; ~65 older videos got 1–54 views each.

| Video | Dur. | Views | Avg view | Avg % viewed | Subs / 1K |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Toy | 27 s | 34.7K | 14.7 s | 55 % | 1.59 |
| Guilty look | 26 s | 32.4K | 12.8 s | 49 % | 0.99 |
| Eyes | 22 s | 28.8K | 10.8 s | 49 % | 0.24 |
| Bathroom | 25 s | 27.2K | 14.9 s | 60 % | 1.88 |
| Stare | 22 s | 24.1K | 11.6 s | 53 % | 0.21 |
| Tears | 16 s | 23.3K | 9.1 s | 57 % | 1.38 |
| Leash (story) | 31 s | 4.1K | 12.6 s | 41 % | 2.43 |
| Bullet (story) | 27 s | 3.6K | 8.9 s | 33 % | 1.11 |

1. "Why your dog does X when you…" explainers: 6 of 6 at 23–35K. The third-person sad stories: 3.6–4.1K.
2. Subscribers come from **you-and-him moments**: toy + bathroom are 35 % of views and 69 % of subscribers; eyes + stare
   are 30 % of views and 8 % of subscribers.
3. Average viewing time is 9–15 s whatever the length, so the first payoff has to arrive early and the second half needs
   its own reason to stay.
4. Reach comes in 1–3 day bursts and stops. Days with no fresh video fell to 0.04–3K views. Judge a video at 72 hours
   (guilty went 3.5K → 18.5K on day 1; bathroom 1.3K → 11.4K on day 2).
5. Longer was not worse: 25–27 s videos averaged 31K, 16–22 s ones 25K (six videos, weak evidence).
6. Retention alone did not break the ceiling (bathroom had the best % viewed at 60 % and 27K views), so more reach needs
   more *sharing and replays*, not just a longer watch.

## 5. The script formula (v3)

**Subject.** An everyday you-and-your-dog moment that every owner has seen, with a surprising explanation behind it.
**Length.** 20–27 s, ~75–95 words at a fast, clear pace (~3.6–3.9 words/s). One idea per sentence.

| Time | Beat | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1.5 s | **Hook** | A command or a half-known puzzle about *your* dog ("Watch your dog."). Cute hero in frame 1. |
| 1.5–6 s | **The behaviour** | What you do → what he does. Something to test. |
| 6–11 s | **Payoff #1** | The explanation with one concrete number or surprise. Aim for the **awe/amusement jolt** here. |
| 11–19 s | **Payoff #2** | The twist about *them*: owner vs stranger, the part that is about this dog. A second reason to stay and a second line worth sharing. |
| 19–25 s | **Warm landing + loop** | One warm line, then the unfinished sentence that runs into the hook. No sad ending. |

Pictures: one idea per place, a visible change every 2–3 s, smooth camera, one small label at a time, captions always on
screen, first frame = last frame. Voice: hedge what the science only half-supports; the owner is never angry.
Title: "Why your dog ___ when you ___" + one 🐶; titles do not drive these views, so don't over-test them.
Publishing: one video a day when possible, never more than two days without one.

## 6. Fixed rules and experiments (we do not have to follow every rule)

**Fixed:** every claim traces to section 1 or a new row; hedged wording; no angry owner; real SFX; seamless loop.

**Experiments for the 100K push (run one change at a time, compare at 72 h, log in the table at the bottom):**
1. **Emotion:** make payoff #1 an awe/amusement jolt, not a calm explanation (tail wag direction, "he knows how long you
   were gone").
2. **Replays:** one hard-loop video of 12–16 s where the last word flows into the first, to test whether replays (now
   counted as views) beat the 25 s format.
3. **Hook:** one video whose first line is a curiosity gap instead of a command ("Your dog's tail wags to the left or
   right, and it means something").
4. **Wildcard slot:** every 4th video may break any non-fixed rule (length, structure, topic). If it beats 35K, it
   becomes a rule.

## 7. Before rendering: the 100K checklist

Hook inside 1.5 s? · Something the viewer can test today? · First payoff by ~8 s? · Awe/amusement/warm jolt, not sad? ·
One repeatable fact? · A second payoff for the people still watching at 12 s? · Every claim sourced and hedged? ·
Loop seamless? · Which experiment from section 6 does this video test?

## 8. Next topics (each with its study; recheck the paper before scripting)

1. **Which way he wags** (Quaranta et al. 2007, *Current Biology*, 30 dogs): the owner drew the strongest right-biased
   wag; an unfamiliar dominant dog drew a left bias. Very visual, great awe jolt.
2. **He knows how long you've been gone** (Rehn & Keeling 2011, *Applied Animal Behaviour Science*, 12 dogs): after 2 and
   4 hours alone, dogs showed higher heart rates and more arousal at the reunion than after 30 minutes.
3. **Why he sleeps on your dirty clothes** (Berns et al. 2015, *Behavioural Processes*, 12 dogs): the caudate reward area
   responded most to the scent of a familiar person; the cause (food, play, bond) is open.
4. **Your words and your tone** (Andics et al. 2016, *Science*, 13 dogs): words and tone are processed in different
   brain sides; reward activity needed praise words in a praising tone. Also answers the "guilty look" audience.
5. **The head tilt** (Sommese et al. 2021, *Animal Cognition*): "gifted word learner" dogs (a handful of border collies)
   tilted their heads far more when they heard a toy's name. Only claim "may mean he is processing a word he knows".
