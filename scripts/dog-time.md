# Your dog knows how long you've been gone

Seamless-loop Short · ~24.4 s (the recorded voiceover) · 96 words · fast pace (~3.9 words/s) · 1080×1920, 60 fps · **toon style** (flat 2D people in a 3D cartoon world)

A relatable "you leave, you come home" moment, one simple fact (he notices how long you were away), one surprising idea (he may be *smelling* time), and a warm ending. No on-screen text: highlights only.

## Voiceover (record exactly this)

> Your dog can tell how long you've been gone.
> Come home after thirty minutes, and he's happy. Come home after two hours, and he goes wild.
> In one study, dogs were more excited after two hours apart than after thirty minutes.
> But he doesn't own a clock. So how does he know?
> Some researchers think he smells time. Your scent fades from the house, and the weaker it gets, the longer you've been gone.
> So that wild welcome? It's his way of saying, "I waited for you."
> So the next time you walk out the door,

The last line is **deliberately unfinished** and runs into the first line on the loop:
*"So the next time you walk out the door, **your dog can tell how long you've been gone.**"* A first-time viewer hears a complete, curious opener.

## Why this script

| Finding | Choice |
|---|---|
| "Why your dog does X when you…" explainers are the format (6 of 6 at 23–35K). | A second-person moment: you leave and come home. |
| Subscribers come from you-and-him moments (the best two: when you come home, when you leave). | The whole video is about your coming and going; the last line is about *you*. |
| Viewers leave around 9–15 s. | The reveal ("he knows how long") is in the first 2 s, the proof by 9 s, the surprise ("he smells time") by 14 s. |
| High-arousal positive emotion spreads; a quiet sad ending does not. | The wild welcome (awe and joy), a funny moment (the dog and the clock), a warm ending. The waiting is never shown as sad. |
| Replays count as views now. | Sentence-wrap loop and a matching first and last frame. |
| Formula experiments: an emotion-first payoff, a curiosity-gap hook, and a wildcard humour beat. | The hook is a claim with a gap ("how?"), and the clock gag is the funny beat. |
| Share trigger: something people can test. | "How long are you gone before the big welcome?" is something every owner can answer (pinned comment). |

## Fact-check (keep claims inside what was found)

- **He notices how long you were away.** Rehn & Keeling 2011, *Applied Animal Behaviour Science* (12 dogs left alone for 30 minutes, 2 hours and 4 hours): after the longer absences (2 and 4 hours) the dogs showed higher heart rates and more excited greeting behaviour than after 30 minutes. It was a small study; it did not find more excitement at 4 hours than at 2.
- **"He smells time" is a hypothesis, not a finding.** Alexandra Horowitz (*Being a Dog*, 2016) suggests dogs may judge how long you've been gone from how faint your scent has become, since weaker smells are usually older. She calls it a hypothesis, and no published study has tested it directly. Say "some researchers think" and "may", never "scientists found".
- **Do not say:** "dogs can tell time exactly", "he counts the minutes", "proves", "all dogs". The line "I waited for you" is a warm metaphor, like "hugging you with his eyes" in `dog-stare`; keep it as feeling, not science.
