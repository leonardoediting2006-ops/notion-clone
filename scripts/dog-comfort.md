# Why your dog drops a slobbery toy in your lap when you cry

Seamless-loop Short · ~35.5 s (the recorded voiceover, `Compound_Clip_9.srt`) · ~105 words · 1080×1920, 60 fps · **toon style** (flat 2D people in a 3D cartoon park) · 24 shots, a cut every ~1.5 s, the camera always moving and always eased.

**What this video is about, in one sentence:** when you cry, your dog brings you his most precious thing, and that is not an invitation to play, it is a gift. Every line is something you can *see* on screen at the same moment, and there is no text on screen (only the word-by-word captions): icons, thought bubbles, a rainbow and a crown do the explaining.

## Voiceover (the recorded one, word for word)

> Your dog isn't actually asking to play fetch. When you're crying or completely overwhelmed and they walk over to drop a dirty, slobbery toy directly into your lap, you probably think they're oblivious to your pain. You see, in their world, they're performing emergency care. Dogs don't understand human problems like heartbreak, grief, or burnout. What they do understand is that that chewed up, ruined toy is their ultimate source of safety, dopamine, and comfort. In their entire existence, it is their single most prized possession. When they drop it in your hands, they aren't asking you to throw it. They're giving away the only thing they own that makes them happy, desperately hoping it will fix whatever is hurting you, which is why.

The last line runs into the first on the loop: *"…whatever is hurting you, which is why **your dog isn't actually asking to play fetch.**"* The closing frame is the opening frame (the golden dog holding the chewed bunny out to the lens).

## Fact-check (keep claims inside what is known)

- The script's central claims (the dog is "performing emergency care", the toy is a source of "dopamine") are an **interpretation**, not a research finding. The description should say "the idea is…" or "many dog people think…", never "scientists found".
- What is documented: dogs notice and move toward a crying person more than toward a person who is talking or humming (Custance & Mayer, *Animal Cognition*, 2012, "Empathic-like responding by domestic dogs to distress in humans: an exploratory study"; small sample, and the authors themselves explain it as emotional contagion plus a learning history, and they do not claim it proves empathy).
- Dogs often bring a toy as an invitation to play or as a greeting; offering it to a sad person as comfort is a common owner interpretation that has not been tested. Say "may".
- Do not say: "dogs understand sadness", "proves", "all dogs", or any medical claim about grief.
