# What holds attention: the brain science and how to write for it

Research reference for writing voiceover scripts for short videos. Compiled October 2026 from about 150 studies, each checked against its abstract or full text where it could be retrieved.

**Evidence tags:** **[A]** meta-analysis, replicated, or causal evidence in humans · **[B]** several studies that agree, or strong animal data plus correlational human data · **[C]** a single or small study, or theory · **[H]** a practitioner rule of thumb that hasn't been tested.

No study has tested any of this on voiceover-only Shorts. The writing rules are inferences from the evidence. Your retention graph is the final judge.

**Contents:** 0 Claims that don't hold up · 1 What the brain is doing · 2 How to write for the ear · 3 Toolkit, myths, sources · Appendix: the first six scripts, measured

## 0. Popular claims that are wrong or overstated

| Claim | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| Dopamine peaks at about 50% outcome uncertainty | This comes from one monkey study. A slow rise in firing over a few seconds peaked at 50% odds, but only 29% of 188 neurons showed it (Fiorillo 2003). It may be an artifact of averaging trials (Niv 2005). For *suspense*, the classic experiment found the peak when a liked hero's odds looked like **1 in 100**, not 50/50 (Comisky & Bryant 1982). |
| When a loop closes, dopamine drops to zero and the viewer leaves | There's no evidence for this. A payoff the brain fully predicted produces no burst, because the response has already moved to the cue. A promised payoff that never arrives produces a dip below baseline (Schultz 1997). No study links closing a loop to people leaving. |
| Cortisol tracks moment-to-moment tension | Cortisol changes over minutes. Second-to-second tension shows up in heart rate, skin conductance and pupil size. One ad study didn't measure cortisol because ads are "too short" to change it (Lin 2013). |
| Oxytocin dissolves psychological distance and predicts donations | In that same ad study, natural oxytocin didn't rise on average (p = .94). The 56% increase in giving came from *nasal-spray* oxytocin given to 40 men. Blood oxytocin tests are unreliable, and oxytocin–trust findings replicate poorly (McCullough 2013; Nave 2015). |
| The listener's brain fires 1–3 s ahead of the speaker | Listeners mostly *lag* the speaker by 1–3 s. A few regions ran ahead, and the more a listener's brain did this, the better they understood (r = .75; 11 listeners, one story; Stephens 2010). The 2024 "herding" paper found the speaker leads by about 6 s and didn't test prediction. |
| Zeigarnik: unfinished questions stay active in working memory | A 2025 meta-analysis found **no** memory advantage for interrupted tasks (recall ratio 0.99). It did find a strong urge to go back and finish them (67%) (Ghibellini & Meier 2025). |
| Working memory for sound holds about 2 s or 7 chunks | Capacity is about 3–5 chunks (Cowan 2001). The "2 seconds" figure is about rehearsing lists of unrelated words, not a buffer for what you hear. |
| Concrete words "directly" fire motor and smell areas; abstract words only fire language areas | Action, smell and taste words do activate related brain regions in some studies. Whether that's needed to understand them is debated (Mahon & Caramazza 2008), and abstract words activate emotional regions. |
| "But/therefore" maps onto how the brain integrates over time | An overclaim. The real support is research on cause-and-effect: events with more causal links are recalled better (Trabasso & van den Broek 1985; Lee & Chen 2022). |
| Monotonous sentence length "lulls" the brain's alertness system | No study shows this. What is supported: sudden changes in voice, sound or pitch grab attention, that effect fades with repetition, and a flat delivery lowers arousal and sometimes memory. |

## 1. What the brain is doing

### 1.1 The model in one paragraph

The brain works by constantly predicting what comes next. A story, a song or a joke plays a structured game with that habit: it sets up something that matters, invites a guess, then confirms it, delays it or overturns it. Across this research, people stay engaged when three things are true at once:

1. **Something valuable is pending.** The brain treats information as a reward and keeps "wanting" while it expects a payoff.
2. **The gap feels answerable.** They know enough to guess, and they believe the answer is coming.
3. **The story holds together.** The parts of the brain that build meaning need it to stay coherent for tens of seconds.

Break any one of these and attention drifts. It drifts more easily when listening than when reading.

### 1.2 Dopamine: what it actually does

**It signals surprise relative to expectation [A].** In monkeys, an unexpected juice reward makes 55–80% of the midbrain's dopamine neurons fire a burst. Once a cue reliably predicts the juice, the burst moves to the cue and the juice itself produces nothing. If the expected juice doesn't arrive, firing dips below baseline at the moment it was due (Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997). Human learning signals follow the same rule and shift with dopamine drugs (Pessiglione 2006).

What this means for a script:
- **The promise carries the anticipation.** That's the hook, the setup, the "here's why." A payoff the listener fully predicted is worth almost nothing.
- **Every payoff needs something they didn't predict**: a detail, a reason, or a consequence they didn't see coming.
- **A broken promise teaches the brain to ignore your cue.** Cues that predict "no reward" come to suppress dopamine neurons (Tobler 2003). That's a plausible reason hooks lose power on channels that underdeliver. In field data, clickbait-style posts got fewer shares than comparable posts, and people who felt manipulated thought less of the source (Mukherjee 2022).

**Wanting is not the same as liking [B].** Rats with about 99% of their dopamine destroyed still show normal "liking" reactions to sugar but lose the drive to go after it. Dopamine drives *wanting*. Pleasure depends heavily on the brain's opioid system (Berridge & Robinson 1998; Berridge & Kringelbach 2015). In humans the split is less clean: a dopamine-boosting drug raised musical pleasure and chills, and a dopamine-blocking drug lowered them (Ferreri 2019). Best summary: dopamine is a learning-and-motivation signal that also tunes pleasure.

→ The hook creates wanting; the payoff has to deliver liking. A strong hook can't rescue a weak payoff, and repeated weak payoffs teach the audience to stop wanting.

**Dopamine rises as a goal gets closer [B, rats].** Dopamine in the striatum ramps up as a rat approaches a distant reward (Howe 2013) and tracks the value of continuing to work (Hamid 2016). Release in the nucleus accumbens can rise with expectation even when dopamine-cell firing doesn't change. In the authors' words: "broadcast burst signals promote learning, whereas local control drives motivation" (Mohebi 2019).

→ A sense of progress toward the answer plausibly keeps people going. Visible progress markers ("the second night…", "the third clue…") are a reasonable translation, but untested.

**Information is a reward in itself [B].**
- Monkeys pick a cue that tells them their reward early 80–100% of the time, even though it doesn't change the reward. The same dopamine neurons respond to expected reward and expected information (Bromberg-Martin & Hikosaka 2009).
- People pay for information that changes nothing. They want news about possible gains (88% of the time) more than about losses (80%). On some trials they paid to *avoid* knowing, depending on whether the news was likely to be good or bad (Charpentier, Bromberg-Martin & Sharot 2018).
- People accepted the risk of an electric shock to learn how a magic trick worked. Their brain activity looked like choosing food when hungry (Lau 2020).
- People open uncertain-but-unpleasant options more often than certain ones, then feel worse afterwards (the "Pandora effect"; Hsee & Ruan 2016).

→ Curiosity is a drive, not decoration. A gap about a threat *to the listener* can make them avoid it. A gap about a *character's* fate, or a surprising "why", pulls more cleanly.

**What makes a good gap [B].**
- **Curiosity is highest at middling confidence.** It peaks when people are about 50% sure they know the answer and is low when they're clueless or certain. High curiosity activated reward-related brain areas, dilated pupils just before the answer, and improved memory for answers people had guessed wrong (Kang 2009).
- **Curiosity boosts memory for the answer.** In high-curiosity states, reward areas in the midbrain and nucleus accumbens activated during the wait. Recall of the answers rose from 54% to 71% an hour later, and from 28% to 46% a day later. Unrelated faces shown during the wait got only about a 4-point boost (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014). At scale the curiosity–memory link is real but modest (r ≈ .10, n = 1,498; Fastrich 2018).
- **Being kept in the dark is mildly unpleasant.** Raising curiosity activated areas linked to discomfort, and resolving it activated reward and memory areas and improved recall (Jepma 2012). Another study found no reward signal at the answer at all (van Lieshout 2018), so a payoff doesn't automatically feel good.
- **Better-than-expected answers stick.** Answers that turned out more interesting than people expected were remembered best (Marvin & Shohamy 2016).
- **Teasing works, though nobody chooses it.** Across seven studies, people who got a gap first and the answer second enjoyed it more than people given everything at once. Yet people don't choose to be teased (Ruan, Hsee & Lu 2018).
- **The PACE model ties this together** (Gruber & Ranganath 2019). The brain notices a gap and judges whether it's manageable. If it is, it becomes curiosity, which drives exploring for the answer. If it feels threatening, it becomes anxiety instead. Each answer can open the next gap.

→ Give a foothold before the gap. Make the gap feel answerable. Put what you want remembered *inside the answer*. Make the answer better than the guess.

**Novelty fades fast [B].** The midbrain responds to novelty itself, and the response drops after a couple of exposures (Bunzeck & Düzel 2006). In monkeys, dopamine novelty responses last "only a few presentations" (Schultz 1997).

→ A device that hooked people last week hooks fewer this week. Rotate formats.

**Music, poetry and humor are the closest analogs to voiceover [B].**
- **Music.** Brain scans showed dopamine release during favorite music. In the same people, one reward area was active in the ~15 s *before* an emotional peak and another *at* the peak. Dopamine release tracked how intense the chills were (r ≈ .8; 8 people picked because they get strong chills; Salimpoor 2011).
- **Surprise needs a confident expectation.** Musical pleasure was highest when a confident expectation was broken, or when an unpredictable stretch resolved cleanly (Cheung 2019, analysis of 80,943 chords).
- **Recited poetry gave all 27 listeners chills.** The chills clustered at the ends of lines, stanzas and poems, and when the poem spoke directly to "you". Reward-area activity rose in the ~4 s *before* each chill (Wassiliwizky 2017). This is the most transferable finding for voiceover.
- **Humor.** Funny cartoons activated reward areas, more so the funnier they were (Mobbs 2003).

→ Build each emotional peak over a few seconds and land it at the end of a sentence or section, ideally addressed to "you". One great joke beats three okay ones.

### 1.3 The other emotion systems

| System | Role in a story | Timescale | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arousal (norepinephrine) | Fires at salient, surprising, decision-relevant moments; a low steady mode goes with drifting attention (Aston-Jones & Cohen 2005). Pupil size tracks it roughly (Joshi 2016). | under a second to seconds | B |
| Body arousal (heart rate, skin conductance) | Rises in charged scenes. In 102 people, audiobook scenes raised heart rate, skin conductance and skin temperature more than the film versions, even though people *rated* the films as more engaging (Richardson 2020; small effects). | seconds | B |
| Cortisol (stress hormone) | Slow. Driven by threats people can't control or that involve being judged (meta-analysis of 208 studies; Dickerson & Kemeny 2004). Not a beat-by-beat signal. | minutes | A (timescale) |
| Oxytocin | Empathy went with a 47% rise in blood oxytocin in one study (Barraza & Zak 2009) and no average rise in another (Lin 2013). How it's measured is disputed. | unclear | C |
| The brain's opioids ("liking") | A tragic film raised pain thresholds (+13%, vs −5% after documentaries) and group bonding (Dunbar 2016; opioids inferred, not measured). Laughing with friends released opioids on brain scans (12 men; Manninen 2017). | minutes | C |
| Carried-over arousal | Leftover arousal makes the next emotion stronger when people don't attribute it to its real source (Zillmann; Cantor, Zillmann & Bryant 1975). | seconds to minutes | B (mechanism) |

Emotion also makes audiences respond in sync. During films, negative emotion made viewers' emotion and self-reflection networks respond more alike, and arousal did the same for sensory and attention networks (Nummenmaa 2012). Emotional spoken stories did the same for listeners (Nummenmaa 2014). Moment-by-moment engagement ratings for *Sherlock* and the audio drama *Paranoia* rose and fell together across people, driven by emotional content (Song, Finn & Rosenberg 2021).

→ Tension followed by release right after the peak is the shape the body supports. Measure it with your retention graph, not with hormones.

### 1.4 The "transported" brain

**Audiences' brains sync up [B].** Tightly edited film makes different people's brains respond alike. Hitchcock's *Bang! You're Dead* aligned over 65% of the cortex, *Curb Your Enthusiasm* 18%, and an unedited shot of a park under 5% (Hasson 2008, figures from the lab's summary). Agreement across a small group's brainwaves predicted Nielsen ratings (r = .60), tweet volume (r = .40), and a 7,000-person panel's ratings of Super Bowl ads (r = .81) (Dmochowski 2014). For StoryCorps videos on YouTube, it predicted real audience engagement (r = .56) and willingness to keep watching (r = .31) (Cohen, Henin & Parra 2017). Paying attention increases this syncing (Ki 2016), and so do powerful speeches (Schmälzle 2015). Syncing means shared processing, though, not proof of understanding.

**Meaning needs coherence over tens of seconds [B].** Researchers scrambled a narrated story at different scales. Hearing areas still responded reliably to scrambled words. Mid-level areas needed intact sentences (about 8 s). The areas that build overall meaning only responded reliably to intact paragraphs (about 38 s) or the whole story (Lerner 2011).

→ A list of disconnected facts keeps the hearing areas busy but never engages the network that builds meaning. Write in connected blocks, with callbacks.

**The first line frames everything [B].** Two groups heard the same 11-minute story after different one-line premises: in one, the wife is having an affair; in the other, the husband is paranoid. The premise changed how their brains processed the rest of the story (Yeshurun 2017).

→ Your first line isn't just a hook. It's the lens for everything after it.

**Boundaries are where memory forms and where people leave [B].** The brain chunks experience into events, and the boundaries fall where predictions break (Zacks 2007). At major boundaries the memory system stores the event that just ended, which predicts later recall (Baldassano 2017). The bigger the boundary, the bigger that response (Ben-Yakov & Henson 2018). Boundaries are also where people act. Spotify skip timing follows song sections (Montecchio 2020). In online course videos, 61% of the spikes in pausing, seeking and rewatching happened at visual transitions (Kim 2014).

→ When one question closes, the next must already be open.

**Characters' minds pull people in [B].** Understanding stories uses the same brain network as working out what other people think (Mar 2011).

→ Characters with visible goals, beliefs and *mistaken* beliefs engage that network, including a dog's imagined beliefs. Dramatic irony ("we know what he doesn't") feeds it directly.

**Words can evoke the senses [C].** Action words like lick, pick and kick activate motor areas for the matching body part (Hauk 2004). "Cinnamon" activates smell areas (González 2006). Stories activate visual and motor areas as events change (Speer 2009). Whether this is needed to understand words is debated (Mahon & Caramazza 2008), and one study found no such effect for smell words (Speed & Majid 2018). Saying "you" or "I" puts readers inside the action, and "he" puts them outside it (Brunyé 2009).

**Stories beat explanations [A].** Being absorbed in a story reduces counter-arguing and shifts beliefs toward the story (Green & Brock 2000). A meta-analysis found absorption is predicted by a plot people can picture (ρ = .29), plausibility (.27) and identifiable characters (.20) (van Laer 2014). Across 33,078 participants, stories beat explanatory text for memory (g = .72) and comprehension (g = .48), whether read or heard (Mar 2021). The popular claim that "stories are 22× more memorable" has no traceable source.

**Minds wander, especially when listening [B].** People are off-task in 47% of sampled waking moments (Killingsworth & Gilbert 2010). Listening to a passage caused more mind-wandering than reading it silently (50.6% vs 35.6%) and worse memory (Varao Sousa 2013). Mind-wandering grows over the course of a lecture (Risko 2012).

### 1.5 Reading vs listening vs watching

| | Reading | Listening | Watching |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who sets the pace | Reader | Speaker | Editor |
| Can go back | Yes | No | Rarely |
| Meaning | Same brain maps as listening (Deniz 2019) | Same as reading | Plus visual information |
| What grabs attention | Layout, typography | Voice changes, sounds, pitch, silence | Cuts, motion, faces |
| Where emotion comes from | Words + inner voice | Words + how they're spoken (speed, loudness, pitch) | Words + faces, music, images |
| Mind-wandering | Lowest (35.6% when reading silently) | Higher (50.6%) | Varies |
| Body response | Not measured | Can exceed video (Richardson 2020) | Rated as more engaging |
| Imagery | Self-generated | Self-generated, steered by the voice | Supplied |
| Main risk | Skimming | Losing the thread with no way back | Passive watching |

→ In audio-first content, the script has to do the editor's job with words: tell people where to look, mark when something changes, and keep a question open.

## 2. How to write scripts that hold people by ear

### 2.1 The engagement loop

Every few seconds in a Short (about every minute in long-form), the script should complete one cycle:

| Step | What the brain is doing | What the script does |
|---|---|---|
| **Promise** | The cue starts to carry anticipation | Signal that something worth knowing is coming |
| **Gap** | Notices a gap it can partly fill and judges it answerable | Name a specific, answerable missing piece |
| **Progress** | Keeps leaning in; suspense is the odds shifting | Each line shifts the odds or narrows the answer |
| **Payoff** | Surprise, relief, and the memory gets stored | Deliver something better than the guess |
| **Next gap** | Wants to finish, so it doesn't leave at the boundary | Open the next question before, or as, you close this one |

### 2.2 The first 1–3 seconds

**Why it's the gate:**
- About 24% of Spotify plays are skipped in the first 5 seconds (Lamere 2014).
- On TV, viewers are most likely to look away in the first second or so. The longer they keep looking, the steadier their attention becomes ("attentional inertia"; Burns & Anderson 1993).
- MrBeast's production guide: "the first minute has the most loss (go look)."
- YouTube Studio reports, for each Short, how many viewers stayed to watch versus swiped away.

**The first line has five jobs:**
1. **Grab attention.** The voice starting, or a change in sound, triggers an automatic attention response (Potter 2008).
2. **Frame.** Say what kind of story this is and whose side to be on (Yeshurun 2017; disposition theory).
3. **Open a specific gap with a foothold.** They must know enough to guess (Kang 2009).
4. **Signal value.** The answer has to sound worth having.
5. **Be true.** The payoff has to beat the promise.

Hook types (examples marked * are made up for illustration, not facts):

| Type | Structure | Why it works | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome first | Give the ending, hold back how or why | Curiosity structure | "This dog took a bullet for a girl he'd met three days earlier." (dog-hero) |
| Bomb under the table | Show a danger the character can't see | Suspense + dramatic irony | *"She didn't know the man at the door had been watching the house for a week." |
| Belief flip | "You think X. It's Y." | Breaks a prediction + it's about the viewer | "Your dog isn't following you into the bathroom because he's clingy." |
| Hidden motive | Give a surprising intention | Engages mind-reading + breaks an expectation | "Your dog evolved a muscle just to manipulate you." (dog-eyes) |
| Concrete oddity | A strange detail you can picture | Gap + imagery | "Every night, my rescue dog slept by the door. With his leash in his mouth." |
| Long odds | A liked character with a slim chance | Suspense peaks at long but nonzero odds | *"The vet gave him one night." |
| Countdown | Deadline + stakes | Leaning in + suspense | *"He had forty seconds before the water reached the door." |
| Question with a foothold | A question they can almost answer | Curiosity peaks at middling confidence | "Why does your dog go wild some days and barely look up on others?" |

Common failures:
- Setup comes before the gap, so no question exists at 3 s.
- Greetings and throat-clearing.
- Abstract words.
- Vague teasers ("you won't believe this").
- Promises the payoff can't keep.

### 2.3 Curiosity engineering

1. **Make the gap specific.** "Why did he growl at the window every night?" beats "Something strange happened."
2. **Choose between *what* and *how/why*.** A *what* question is dead once it's answered. A *how* or *why* question survives even when the outcome is known. Spoiler research is mixed: one study found spoilers *increased* enjoyment of short stories (n = 819; Leavitt & Christenfeld 2011), while later studies found less enjoyment or no effect (Johnson & Rosenbaum 2015; Levine 2016; Daniel & Katz 2019). Reveal the *what* when it hooks; keep the *how/why* open.
3. **Relay loops; don't stack them.** Open the next loop before closing the current one, in the same breath: "…which explains the growling. It doesn't explain the third night."
4. **Rely on the urge to finish, not on memory.** People reliably want to resume unfinished things (67%), but they don't remember them better (Ghibellini & Meier 2025). Unfinished goals also keep intruding on thought until a plan forms (Masicampo & Baumeister 2011).
5. **Put the message in the answer.** Curiosity boosts memory for the answer itself by 16–18 points, and for nearby material by only about 4 (Gruber 2014).
6. **Overdeliver.** Answers that beat expectations are remembered best (Marvin & Shohamy 2016).
7. **Tease, briefly.** Opening a gap and then closing it beats flat delivery (Ruan 2018). But being kept in the dark is mildly unpleasant (Jepma 2012), so keep each gap short and close it cleanly.
8. **Save cliffhangers for series.** They raised desire for the next installment in two studies (Schibler, Hahn & Green 2023). In another, they raised arousal but not enjoyment or intent to keep watching (Wirz 2023).

### 2.4 Suspense, curiosity and surprise are different structures

| Structure | What the audience knows | Order | Feels like | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Suspense | The danger | Danger early, outcome delayed | "Will it happen?" | Rescues, danger, contests |
| Curiosity | The outcome | Outcome first, how/why later | "How did it come to this?" | True stories, explainers |
| Surprise | Nothing seems to be missing | Hidden fact revealed late | "Wait, what?" | Twists, reframes |

People liked stories more when they had one of these patterns and it was resolved (Brewer & Lichtenstein 1982).

- **Suspense beats surprise.** Hitchcock on the bomb under the table: "In the first case we have given the public fifteen seconds of surprise at the moment of the explosion. In the second we have provided them with fifteen minutes of suspense."
- **Make them like the protagonist first, then make the odds long but not zero.** Suspense peaked at roughly 1-in-100 odds and rose with how much people liked the hero (Comisky & Bryant 1982).
- **Suspense survives a known outcome.** After a story put an obstacle in front of a famous outcome (Washington becoming president), readers were slower to confirm what they already knew (Gerrig 1989). That's useful for history and explainer Shorts.
- **Shift the odds every beat and resolve at the end.** In a formal model, suspense is highest when each beat shifts belief a little, the decisive reveal is held to the end, and twists grow bigger but rarer (Ely, Frankel & Kamenica 2015). A measure based on that idea matched human suspense ratings almost as well as people match each other (τ = .62 vs .65; Wilmot & Keller 2020).
- **A twist needs a setup.** In music, surprise was most pleasurable when it broke a *confident* expectation, not when everything was already unpredictable (Cheung 2019). A twist that changes the meaning of earlier lines pays off; a random one doesn't.

### 2.5 Emotional design

- **Alternate.** Bigger swings between positive and negative emotion predicted higher movie ratings and more reading-on across 32,085 online articles. In experiments, alternating positive and negative scenes raised liking (in one study only among thrill-seekers). Extreme swings overwhelm (Berger, Kim & Meyer 2021).
- **Surprise early, then joy.** In video ads, surprise focused attention and joy kept viewers from skipping. How strong the emotion was mattered, not how fast it changed (Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2012).
- **Build one peak and end strong.** People's memory of a clip is dominated by its peak (r ≈ .77) and its end (r ≈ .70); length barely matters (Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993). How much people like an ad follows its peak, its ending and whether it keeps improving (Baumgartner 1997).
- **Place the chills.** Build for about 4 seconds, land on the end of a line or section, and address "you" (Wassiliwizky 2017).
- **High-arousal emotions spread.** For every step up in awe, an article's odds of making the NYT most-emailed list rose 30%. Anger raised them 34% and anxiety 21%; sadness lowered them 16% (Berger & Milkman 2012). Sad stories need a high-energy turn: awe, relief, hope, or outrage at an injustice.
- **Feature one specific individual.** A single named victim causes the most distress (Kogut & Ritov 2005). But the effect on *giving* is weak (meta-analysis r = .05, and a 2024 preregistered replication failed). Use it for emotion, not as a guaranteed call to action.
- **Humor.** Humor is a violation that also feels safe (McGraw & Warren 2010). Set up a confident assumption, then break it in a way that still makes sense.
- **Arc.** In books, "man in a hole" (fall, then rise) is among the most-downloaded shapes (Reagan 2016). A 30-second Short has room for exactly one fall and rise.

### 2.6 Cause and effect

- **"But" and "therefore," never "and then."** Trey Parker, at an NYU class in 2011: "if the words 'and then' belong between those beats, you're f—ed… What should happen between every beat… is either the word 'therefore' or 'but'." The rule itself hasn't been tested [H]. But events with more causal links are judged more important and remembered better (Trabasso & van den Broek 1985). In films, how many causal links a scene had predicted recall (r = .29; Lee & Chen 2022).
- **The story spine.** Kenn Adams' template, popularized through Pixar's Emma Coats, is a chain of causes: *Once upon a time… Every day… One day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally…*
- **Ira Glass's two building blocks** (paraphrased). *This American Life*'s host describes the anecdote, a sequence of actions that has momentum even when the material is ordinary, and the moment of reflection that says why it matters. Within the anecdote you keep raising questions that promise answers.
- **Signal every change of time, place, character or goal.** Unannounced shifts slow readers down (Zwaan 1995, 1998), and listeners can't go back and re-read.

### 2.7 Writing for the ear

- **Introduce no more than 3–4 new things before resolving them** (Cowan 2001).
- **Keep connected words close together.** Sentences get harder to process the further apart words that depend on each other are (Gibson 1998). Speakers of all 37 languages studied keep that distance short (Futrell 2015). Put the subject and verb early and together, drop asides in the middle of sentences, and put long phrases last.
  - Before: "Every night, after bringing him home from the shelter, my dog slept by the front door, holding his leash in his mouth."
  - After: "Every night, my rescue dog slept by the front door. With his leash in his mouth."
- **Put the new or important word last.** That's where spoken stress falls naturally.
- **Concrete beats abstract.** Concreteness was "overwhelmingly the best predictor" of interest, comprehension and recall across text types (Sadoski 2000). People also judge concrete wording as more *true* (Hansen & Wänke 2010), which raises your duty to be accurate.
- **Use "you."** It puts the listener inside the scene (Brunyé 2009). Changing "the" to "your" in a narrated lesson helped people apply what they learned, though it didn't help recall (Mayer 2004).
- **Numbers:** one per sentence, rounded, and compared to something familiar.
- **Repeat for emphasis**, as in dog-hero's "Three days earlier. Three days earlier." Repetition makes a line smoother and more believable. It makes statements feel truer even when people know better (Fazio 2015), so only repeat claims that are true.
- **Use rhythm and rhyme for the closing line.** People judged rhyming sayings more accurate than non-rhyming versions with the same meaning (McGlone & Tofighbakhsh 2000).
- **One breath per sentence.** Read it aloud to check.

### 2.8 Delivery: pace, pitch, pauses and sound

| Lever | Evidence | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Pace | For radio news, recognition was best at about 170 words per minute for dense content and 190 for light content. Worst was slow with little content, or fast with dense content (Rodero 2016). Comprehension of simple material holds up to about 250 wpm (Foulke 1968). Young adults lose little at 2× lecture speed (Murphy 2022). Faster, more enthusiastic online-course speakers drew more engagement (Guo 2014; average 156 wpm). | Match pace to how dense and how emotional the line is. Slow down for the reveal and tender moments. |
| Pitch | Varying pitch from sentence to sentence beat a flat delivery for arousal, attention and memory (Rodero, Potter & Prieto 2017). How lively a speaker sounded tracked their pitch variation (r = .83; Hincks 2005). | Write sentences that can rise and fall. Avoid flat text-to-speech. |
| Emphasis | Moderate emphasis beat both none and over-the-top announcer emphasis (Rodero & Potter 2021). A storytelling style held attention better than a fast, emphatic broadcast style (Rodero & Cores-Sarría 2021). | Tell the story; don't announce it. |
| Emotional tone | Speed, loudness and pitch tell listeners how intense the emotion is. Whether it's positive or negative comes mostly from the words (Juslin & Laukka 2003; Banse & Scherer 1996). | Tender = slower, softer, lower. Excited = faster, louder, higher. |
| Pauses | A silent pause before a word made people more likely to remember it later (MacGregor 2010). "Uh" fillers improved recall of the plot compared with coughs (Fraundorf & Watson 2011). | Leave a beat before the key word. Don't scrub out every natural hesitation. |
| Stressing the contrast | Stressing the word that sets up a contrast improved memory for that contrast (Fraundorf 2010). | "He wasn't holding it to *leave*. He was holding it to be *ready*." |
| Voice and sound changes | A change of voice grabbed attention, and so did 8 of 9 other changes in the audio. Both improved memory for what came right after (Potter 2000, 2008). Repeating the same device stopped working by about the third time (Potter 2015). | Use a change at each re-hook. Rotate devices. |
| Sound effects | Specific sound effects that place the scene, and sounds positioned like a camera shot, increased mental imagery (Rodero 2012). Spatial audio increased imagery and absorption, though sound effects alone didn't improve recall (Rodero & Romero 2022). | A few specific sounds at key beats, not a constant wall. |
| Background music | Background speech and lyrics interfere with understanding words the most; instrumental music less; fast, loud music hurts (Salamé & Baddeley 1989; Vasilev 2018). Faster tempo raises arousal, and major vs minor key shifts mood (Husain 2002). | A quiet instrumental bed, lowered under the voice, with no lyrics. Drop it before the payoff line. |
| Human vs synthetic voice | Human narrators beat synthetic voices on enjoyment, imagery, engagement, attention and memory (Rodero & Lucas 2023). | If you use text-to-speech, pick the most expressive voice and script the pauses and emphasis. Newer voices may narrow the gap; that hasn't been tested. |

### 2.9 Beat templates (Shorts of 21–35 s)

**A. Belief flip (~25 s)**
- 0–2 s: the flip as the first line, which opens the gap and frames the story.
- 2–8 s: the explanation, told as a concrete scene.
- 8–16 s: evidence, or a consequence that raises one more question.
- 16–22 s: the turn that answers it, better than expected.
- 22–25 s: a "you" reframe built over about 4 seconds; optionally loop back to the start.

**B. Outcome first (~30 s)**
- 0–3 s: the outcome.
- 3–10 s: the underdog setup (why we like him).
- 10–20 s: a "but/therefore" chain with dramatic irony.
- 20–26 s: the climax with one sensory detail, read slower.
- 26–30 s: close the open questions, then a reflection that reframes the opening.

**C. Experiment reveal (~30 s)**
- 0–3 s: a relatable scene plus the question.
- 3–15 s: the test, as a "but/therefore" sequence.
- 15–24 s: the result, as a concrete image.
- 24–30 s: a personal reframe that loops back to the first line.

**D. 60-second story**
- 0–3 s: hook.
- By 10 s: stakes and a reason to like the character.
- By 20 s: first payoff, and the next question open.
- 30–40 s: reversal.
- 40–52 s: climax against long odds.
- 52–60 s: payoff and reflection, then cut.

### 2.10 Platform mechanics

- **YouTube Shorts:** since 31 March 2025, every start or replay counts as a view, with no minimum watch time. "Engaged views" still determine monetization. YouTube publishes no retention benchmarks, so compare against your own median. Video length alone explains over 58% of the variation in average percentage watched (Wu, Rizoiu & Xie 2018), so only compare Shorts of similar length.
- **TikTok:** its leaked "TikTok Algo 101" document says the system optimizes for "retention" and "time spent". It gives a simplified score of Plike × Vlike + Pcomment × Vcomment + Eplaytime × Vplaytime + Pplay × Vplay (reported by the NYT, 2021). Roughly: the predicted chance of each action (like, comment, watch time, play) times how much the system values it.
- **MrBeast's production guide [H]:**
  - "the first minute has the most loss (go look)"
  - "Stop telling people what they will be watching and start showing them"
  - "crazy progression"
  - a "re-engagement" moment around minute 3 and again around minute 6
  - "Strong payoffs at the end of videos boost retention"
  - "No dull moments"
  - "The video endings must always be abrupt to protect retention."
- **Loop endings [H]:** because replays count as views, an ending that flows back into the first line can earn replays. dog-welcome already does this.

## 3. Toolkit

### 3.1 Write every script as a table

| Time | Line | Open questions after this line | Mechanism | Delivery | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | "This dog took a bullet for a girl he'd met three days earlier." | Why? How? Did he live? | Outcome first | Steady, mid pace | Music in, low |
| 3.5 | "Three days earlier." | (same) | Repetition for emphasis | Slower, falling pitch | — |

If the "open questions" cell is ever empty before the last line, that's where viewers leave.

### 3.2 Pre-flight checklist
1. **3-second test:** read the opening aloud with a stopwatch. Is a specific question open by 3 s?
2. **Frame:** does the first line say what kind of story this is and whose side we're on?
3. **Open questions:** after every sentence, can you name what the listener is waiting for?
4. **"And then" test:** does "and then" fit between any two beats? Rewrite with "but" or "therefore".
5. **Picture test:** can a listener with their eyes closed see, hear or feel something every one or two sentences?
6. **Load:** no more than 3–4 new things before something resolves. Subject and verb together. No asides mid-sentence.
7. **Peak:** one peak, built up and landing at the end of a sentence. The video ends within about a second of the final payoff.
8. **Promise:** does the payoff beat what the hook promised? Is every claim true?
9. **Pace:** words ÷ seconds × 60. Slow down dense and tender lines.
10. **Novelty:** have you used this exact device in your last three videos?

### 3.3 Measuring what works
- Check the gate first: stayed to watch vs swiped away. Then check the retention curve. A drop right after a payoff means the next question wasn't open yet.
- Change one thing at a time, ideally only the first line.
- Compare against your own median for Shorts of similar length, not against internet benchmarks.

### 3.4 Ethics that are also good strategy
- A promise you don't keep teaches your audience to discount your next hook. Clickbait cost shares in real-world data (Mukherjee 2022).
- Concrete wording, repetition and rhyme make claims feel true whether or not they are (Hansen & Wänke 2010; Fazio 2015; McGlone & Tofighbakhsh 2000). Use them only on true claims.
- Curiosity about bad outcomes can leave people feeling worse (Hsee & Ruan 2016). Pay off the tension with relief.

### 3.5 Myths to drop
- **"We have an 8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish."** The figure has no traceable source, and attention depends on the task (BBC 2017).
- **"Attention collapses after 10 minutes."** The original data don't support it (Wilson & Korn 2007; Bradbury 2016).
- **"Dopamine = pleasure."** It's mainly wanting and learning, though it also tunes pleasure.
- **"Oxytocin is the moral molecule"** and **"mirror neurons explain empathy."** Neither holds up (Nave 2015; Hickok 2009; Heyes & Catmur 2022).
- **"Stories are 22× more memorable."** The measured memory advantage is g = .72, about 0.7 of a standard deviation (Mar 2021).
- **"We remember 10% of what we read, 20% of what we hear…"** The chart's numbers have no reliable source (Subramony 2014).
- **"93% of communication is nonverbal."** A misreading of Mehrabian's 1967 studies (Lapakko 2015).
- **"We can hold 7 ± 2 items."** It's about 4 (Cowan 2001).
- **"Unfinished things are remembered better."** This didn't hold up in the 2025 meta-analysis (Ghibellini & Meier 2025).
