# Five dog Shorts scripts

Written with the rules in [attention-research.md](attention-research.md). Every claim is from a real study or documented history; sources and caveats are under each script.

**How to read the scripts**
- `/` = short pause (about 0.3 s). `//` = full beat (about 1 s).
- **Bold** = stress this word.
- Times assume about 180 words per minute, slower on tender lines.
- "Open question" = what the viewer is waiting for after that line. It should never be empty before the last line.

| # | Title | Structure | Length | Main emotion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The guilty look | Belief flip + experiment | ~34 s | Surprise → empathy |
| 2 | Tears | Experiment reveal | ~42 s | Tenderness → joy |
| 3 | The witness | Experiment with suspense | ~37 s | Loyalty |
| 4 | Togo | True story, outcome first | ~57 s | Awe → injustice → pride |
| 5 | The head tilt | Belief flip + challenge | ~39 s | Curiosity → fun |

## 1. The guilty look

**YouTube title:** Your dog's "guilty look" isn't guilt

### Voiceover

> Your dog's guilty look **isn't** guilt.
> A scientist set a trap to test it.
> Owners told their dogs: don't eat that treat. Then they left the room.
> While they were gone, the scientist either **fed** the dog the treat / or took it **away**.
> Then she told the owners what happened. // But half the time, she **lied**.
> Owners who thought their dog ate it got angry at him. And there it was: ears back. Eyes down.
> Even on dogs that **never** touched it.
> The guiltiest faces of all? // The **innocent** dogs who got yelled at anyway.
> He's not saying sorry. //
> He's reading **you**.

### Beats

| Time | Line | Open question | Delivery and sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–2 s | "Your dog's guilty look isn't guilt." | Then what is it? | Confident, quick. Music bed in, low. |
| 2–4 s | "A scientist set a trap to test it." | What trap? | Lean in. |
| 4–8 s | Owners give the order, leave the room | Will he eat it? | Door closing. |
| 8–13 s | Scientist feeds the treat or takes it away | What's the trick? | Matter-of-fact. |
| 13–17 s | "But half the time, she lied." | What happens when they're lied to? | Beat before "lied". Music drops out. |
| 17–23 s | Getting angry brings out the look | Did the guilty dogs look guiltier? | Slower on "ears back, eyes down". |
| 23–25 s | "Even on dogs that never touched it." | So who looked guiltiest? | Stress "never". |
| 25–29 s | "The innocent dogs who got yelled at anyway." | What is the look, then? | Build for about 4 s; land on "anyway". |
| 29–34 s | "He's not saying sorry. He's reading you." | — (end) | Quiet, close to the mic. Cut on "you". |

**Visuals:** a dog with `pose({ earBack, sad, headPitch })` beside an empty plate; owner avatar walking out and back in; at the twist, a split screen of a dog with an empty plate and a dog with a full plate, both making the same face.

### Sources
- Horowitz 2009, *Behavioural Processes*, 10.1016/j.beproc.2009.03.014. Full text read. Of the four key trials, owners were misinformed in two, so "half the time" is exact. The guilty look showed up more when owners scolded, whether or not the dog had eaten the treat, and "the effect of scolding was more pronounced when the dogs were obedient". Caveat: 14 dogs.
- Supporting: Hecht, Miklósi & Gácsi 2012 found owners couldn't tell from the greeting whether their dog had misbehaved.

## 2. Tears

**YouTube title:** Scientists measured your dog's tears when you come home

### Voiceover

> Scientists measured your dog's **tears** when you come home.
> They tucked a thin paper strip under the eyelid, to measure how wet his eye was.
> First, on a normal day at home. / Then right after his owner walked back in, after hours apart.
> The strips came back **wetter**.
> But dogs get excited by lots of people. / So they tried someone the dog knew well. // Just not the owner.
> The tears didn't rise the **same** way.
> Then they showed people photos of dogs with a few drops of fake tears added.
> People liked the teary-eyed dogs more.
> So when you walk through that door, it isn't just his **tail** saying hello. //
> It's his **eyes**.
> And they do it most / for **you**.

### Beats

| Time | Line | Open question | Delivery and sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 s | "Scientists measured your dog's tears when you come home." | Dogs cry? What did they find? | Warm, curious. Soft piano bed. |
| 3–8 s | The paper strip under the eyelid | What did it show? | Concrete and close. |
| 8–14 s | Normal day vs the moment the owner returns | Was there a difference? | Keys in the lock, door opening. |
| 14–16 s | "The strips came back wetter." | Is it just excitement? | Beat before "wetter". |
| 16–23 s | The familiar-person test | Same result? | Brisk, a slight "gotcha" tone. |
| 23–25 s | "The tears didn't rise the same way." | So it's about the owner. Why would tears matter? | Slow. |
| 25–33 s | The fake-tears photos | What does that mean for me? | Slight lift. |
| 33–42 s | "It's his eyes. And they do it most / for you." | — (end) | Slowest line of the script. Music swells, then cut on "you". |

**Visuals:** close-up of a dog's eyes with a slow push-in; the paper strip as a small prop; front door opening with the owner avatar; at the end, a slight wet sheen on the eyes (raise the eye material's clearcoat).

### Sources
- Murata et al. 2022, *Current Biology*, 10.1016/j.cub.2022.07.031. Dogs' tear volume rose after reunion with their owners, and rose more than after reunion with familiar people who weren't their owners. People rated photos of dogs more positively after artificial tears were added. (The study also tested oxytocin eye drops; the script leaves that out on purpose.)
- Caveats: one small study; press reports say 22 dogs. Some outside experts were cautious about calling this "tears of joy" (Smithsonian, Aug 2022). The script says "measured" and "wetter", not "cry".

## 3. The witness

**YouTube title:** Your dog is watching how people treat you

### Voiceover

> Your dog is watching how people treat **you**.
> Scientists in Kyoto tested it with fifty-four dogs.
> An owner struggles to open a box. / She turns to the man beside her and asks for help.
> He turns **away**.
> A second stranger just sits there, staring at the floor.
> Then **both** strangers hold out the same treat.
> Which hand does the dog pick? //
> When the man had **helped** the owner, dogs didn't care. Either treat was fine.
> But when he **wouldn't help** her… //
> the dogs mostly went to the **other** hand.
> Same treat. He got fed either way.
> He just saw how that man treated you. //
> And he picked a side. //
> **Yours.**

### Beats

| Time | Line | Open question | Delivery and sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 s | "Your dog is watching how people treat you." | Watching how? What does he do? | Low, a little ominous. |
| 3–5 s | "Scientists in Kyoto tested it with fifty-four dogs." | How? | Credible, brisk. |
| 5–10 s | Owner struggles, asks for help | Will he help? | Plastic lid creaking. |
| 10–11 s | "He turns away." | How will the dog react? | Flat. Chair scrape. |
| 11–17 s | Neutral stranger; both hold out a treat | Which hand? | Slow. Music drops out. |
| 17–19 s | "Which hand does the dog pick?" | (the main question) | Full beat after. Silence. |
| 19–23 s | After he helped: no preference | So what about the refuser? | Brisk. |
| 23–28 s | "…the dogs mostly went to the other hand." | Why would he care? | Build; stress "other". |
| 28–33 s | "Same treat… how that man treated you." | — | Gentle. |
| 33–37 s | "And he picked a side. // Yours." | — (end) | Music back in. Cut on "Yours." |

**Visuals:** owner avatar between two seated strangers, the owner struggling with a clear box; the refuser turning his shoulder; both strangers holding out flat palms; the dog looking from one hand to the other, then walking past the refuser. The dog's head yaw does the storytelling.

### Sources
- Chijiiwa et al. 2015, *Animal Behaviour*, 10.1016/j.anbehav.2015.05.018; design from Kyoto University's release. 54 dogs, 18 per condition, 4 trials each. The owner tried to open a clear container holding a roll of tape, and the "non-helper" refused by turning away. A neutral person looked at the floor the whole time. Both offered an identical treat, and the dog was fed whichever hand it chose. Choices were random after the helper and in a control condition. After the refusal, choices were "significantly biased against" the refuser.
- Caveat: this is one lab's paradigm, and a 2023 Yale study (Richards, Silver & Santos) found the dog's attachment affects these choices. The script says "mostly", not "every dog".

## 4. Togo

**YouTube title:** A 12-year-old dog saved a town. Another dog got the statue.

### Voiceover

> A twelve-year-old dog saved a town. // Another dog got the **statue**.
> Nome, Alaska. 1925. A deadly disease is spreading through the children, and the medicine is almost seven hundred miles away.
> The only planes are taken apart for winter. / So twenty dog teams pass it hand to hand.
> The longest, most dangerous run goes to **Togo**.
> As a puppy, he was so sick his owner gave him away. / Togo jumped through a **window** to get back.
> Now he's old for a sled dog. And he has to cross twenty miles of frozen sea. / In the dark. / At thirty below.
> Togo finds the trail. They make it across.
> That night, the ice they crossed **breaks away** and floats out to sea.
> Togo runs two hundred and sixty miles in all.
> But the last fifty-five go to a team led by **Balto**.
> Reporters meet Balto. New York gives **him** a statue.
> Togo's name fades. //
> Twelve years old. / Two hundred and sixty miles. //
> Now you know his **name**.

### Beats

| Time | Line | Open question | Delivery and sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–4 s | "A twelve-year-old dog saved a town. Another dog got the statue." | Which dog? How? Why the injustice? | Firm. Wind ambience. |
| 4–11 s | Nome, 1925, a deadly disease, medicine 674 miles away | How will it get there? | Urgent, clear. |
| 11–15 s | Planes taken apart; twenty dog teams | Who's the hero? | Brisk. |
| 15–18 s | "The longest, most dangerous run goes to Togo." | Can an old dog do it? | Stress "Togo". |
| 18–24 s | Sick puppy, given away, jumps through a window | (you're on his side now) | Warmer, a slight smile. Glass crack. |
| 24–32 s | Twenty miles of frozen sea, dark, thirty below | Will the ice hold? | Slow, short phrases. Wind up, ice creaking. Music out. |
| 32–35 s | "Togo finds the trail. They make it across." | Safe? | Relief. |
| 35–39 s | "That night, the ice they crossed breaks away…" | How close was it? | Hushed. Deep ice crack. |
| 39–42 s | Two hundred and sixty miles | So why isn't he famous? | Proud. |
| 42–49 s | Balto's last 55 miles; reporters; the statue | (the injustice lands) | Flat, a little bitter on "him". |
| 49–51 s | "Togo's name fades." | — | Full beat after. |
| 51–57 s | "Twelve years old… Now you know his name." | — (end) | Music swells under the last line. Cut on "name". |

**Visuals:** snowy night with the sled team in silhouette (procedural snow, the trees module, a box-built sled); for the window beat, a small dog bursting through a pane; ice cracks as a decal on a white plane; Balto's statue as a simple bronze-material dog on a rock.

### Sources
- 674 miles, 20 mushers, about 150 dogs; Seppala ran 170 miles out and carried the serum 91 miles back, 261 in total; Togo was 12; about 20 miles of open Norton Sound ice in darkness at about −30 °F (wind chill about −85 °F); the ice they crossed blew out to sea while they slept; the available planes were dismantled with open cockpits; the Central Park statue was unveiled on 15 Dec 1925. From the Wikipedia article on the 1925 serum run.
- Togo was 12 and led his team over 261 miles. As a puppy he "developed a painful throat disorder"; Seppala gave him up for adoption, and Togo "escaped his adoptee's home by jumping through a window". "Balto ran 55 miles, while Togo's leg of the journey was the longest and most dangerous." From the US National Park Service.
- Caveat: Balto's leg is given as 53 or 55 miles depending on the source; the script says 55, matching the NPS.

## 5. The head tilt

**YouTube title:** Your dog's head tilt might not mean he's confused

### Voiceover

> Your dog's head tilt might not mean he's **confused**.
> Scientists in Budapest filmed forty dogs while their owners asked for a toy by name.
> Most dogs barely tilted. / **Two** percent of the time.
> But a few rare dogs, the ones who'd learned their toys' names, / tilted forty-**three** percent of the time.
> Same question. / About twenty times the tilting.
> The scientists' guess: // the tilt shows up when a word **means** something. / When he's matching what he hears to what he **knows**.
> And here's the strange part. // Each dog tilted to the **same** side, month after month.
> So say the word that means the most to him. //
> Watch his head.
> **Left**… / or **right**?

### Beats

| Time | Line | Open question | Delivery and sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 s | "Your dog's head tilt might not mean he's confused." | Then what does it mean? | Playful, quick. Light pizzicato bed. |
| 3–8 s | Forty dogs, owners asking for a toy by name | What did they see? | Brisk. |
| 8–11 s | "Most dogs barely tilted. Two percent." | So which dogs tilt? | Deadpan. |
| 11–17 s | Rare word-learner dogs: 43% | Why them? | Build; stress "three". |
| 17–19 s | "About twenty times the tilting." | What's going on in his head? | Punchy. |
| 19–27 s | "The tilt shows up when a word means something." | Is that all? | Slower, wondering. |
| 27–31 s | "Each dog tilted to the same side, month after month." | Which side is my dog? | Beat before "same". |
| 31–39 s | "Say the word… Watch his head. Left… or right?" | (answer in the comments) | Smile in the voice. Music out before "Left". Cut on "right?" |

**Visuals:** a dog with `pose({ tilt })` animated left and right; simple on-screen counters ("2%" and "43%") above the YouTube safe zone; at the end, the dog freezes mid-tilt, facing the camera.

### Sources
- Sommese et al. 2021/2022, *Animal Cognition* 25:701–705, 10.1007/s10071-021-01571-8 (open access; full text read). 40 dogs were filmed while owners asked them to fetch a familiar toy by name. The "Gifted Word Learner" dogs tilted their heads in 43% of trials and typical dogs in 2%. The side of the tilt "was stable across several months and tests". The authors "suggest a relationship between head-tilting and processing relevant, meaningful stimuli".
- Caveats: an exploratory study, with only a handful of word-learner dogs. The script says "might" and "the scientists' guess", and the 20× figure is 43 ÷ 2, rounded down.

## Before recording
- **Simple words:** if you wouldn't say a word to a 10-year-old, swap it. Listeners can't rewind.
- **3-second check:** every opening line puts a specific question in the viewer's head by about 3 s.
- **Pace:** scripts 1, 2, 3 and 5 sit at about 170–190 wpm; script 4 is denser, so keep it near 170 and never above 190.
- **Last word:** cut the video within about a second of the final word. Script 5 ends on a question, which trades a loop for comments.
- **Variety:** post them in an order that alternates moods, e.g. 1 → 4 → 2 → 5 → 3.
